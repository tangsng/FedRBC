# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background and motivation

Hospital networks are quietly turning into one of the densest Internet-of-Things (IoT) environments around: wearable telemetry patches, bedside monitors, infusion pumps, and imaging gateways push out a steady stream of traffic that is at once safety-critical and privacy-protected. Federated learning (FL) is a natural match for detecting intrusions in that traffic. In the FedAvg formulation of McMahan et al. [A1], devices train a shared detection model by minimizing the global empirical risk $F(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} p_i F_i(\mathbf{w})$ over $N$ clients with data weights $p_i$ while exchanging only parameter updates — the training loop our Section 4 framework builds on. The clinical appeal of that arrangement has been argued from the digital-health perspective by Rieke et al. [E1], who cast FL as the enabler of multi-institutional model collaboration without patient-data relocation, and demonstrated end-to-end by Kaissis et al. [E2], whose privacy-preserving federated pipeline for medical imaging shows that model quality need not be traded away for data sovereignty — the premise a hospital-edge deployment inherits. For intrusion detection specifically, the pairing has accumulated its own evidence base. Hurt et al. [F1] compare medical- and network-sourced features for healthcare IDS and find network telemetry indispensable, which supports our choice of flow features as the training signal. Recent systems work then matures the pairing across deployment axes: an end-to-end FL-IDS validation on IoT traffic [N0], communication-aware optimization of federated intrusion detection in IoT networks [N1], a community-driven hierarchical design for smart homes (HomeGuard) [N2] whose multi-tier organization parallels hospital ward structures, quantum-resilient detection for the post-quantum threat horizon [N3], incremental learning to track evolving threat landscapes [N4], and distributed intrusion detection across large IoT populations [N5]. What this literature establishes is accuracy under honest participation; it does not address the case in which a *participant* is the adversary.

The engineering reality of hospital IoT, however, is that devices are also the easiest entry point for attackers. Default credentials, unpatchable firmware, and physically accessible ports make device compromise routine rather than exceptional [G1]. Once inside, the adversary's most damaging move is not to steal data but to *corrupt the collective model itself*: by submitting manipulated updates, a few compromised devices can shift the decision boundary of the intrusion detector that every other device relies on — a self-defeating loop in which the defense system becomes the attack vector.

Defending against such Byzantine poisoning is well studied in theory. Krum and its coordinate-wise relatives achieve provable robustness against arbitrary adversary behavior under bounded Byzantine fractions [B1], and the statistical-rate analysis of Yin et al. [B2] establishes trimmed-mean and median rules as minimax-optimal — the filtering family from which our Layer-0 pre-filter descends. Collusion, however, is the theoretical weak point of that family: the LIE construction of Baruch et al. [B6] shows how few coordinated clients suffice to slip past coordinate filtering while remaining direction-aligned — the collusive family our Section 5.9 evaluation adopts — and model-replacement backdoors [B4] demonstrate that a single scaled update can overwrite the global model outright under naive averaging. On the optimization side, the non-IID convergence analysis of FedAvg by Li et al. [H1] is the tradition our Theorem 1 extends to reputation-weighted aggregation, the survey of Kairouz et al. [A2] frames the open problems this work addresses, FedProx [A3] tolerates statistical heterogeneity through proximal regularization, and adaptive federated optimization [A5] relocates the adaptation burden to the server — complementary handles on the non-IID regime our Dirichlet partitioner instantiates. Even so, three gaps remain when this work is translated to a deployable hospital-edge system. First, **collusion breaks plurality-based defenses**: statistical filters (median, trimmed mean, distance voting, and their recent systematizations [B5]) assume that honest updates form a recognizable majority, and a network-level adversary controlling several devices forges that majority. Second, **detection without actuation is operationally hollow**: most robust-FL work ends at a score or a weight, and nothing in the network changes — in a hospital, a detected malicious infusion pump must stop talking to its peers, promptly and provably. Third, **trust has no memory**: per-round scoring [N11] forgets everything between rounds, so an adversary can behave intermittently and never accumulate consequences.

## 1.2 Related work and positioning

Three research lines meet at our problem. **Blockchain-enabled FL** couples ledgers with learning for auditability and incentive alignment. DeepChain [C1] introduced auditable gradient commitments, tying each training step to a signed on-chain record; FedRBC inherits the audit ideal but commits scalar evidence rather than gradients, cutting the per-round chain payload to bytes rather than model-sized blobs (Section 4.1). Follow-ups anchor the pattern across domains: privacy-preserved data sharing in industrial IoT [C2], vehicular analytics with an explicit analysis of design challenges [C3], committee-consensus decentralization of the federation itself [C4], and blockchained on-device learning that pushes ledger bookkeeping onto end devices [C5]. A second wave manages the blockchain-FL resource stack: trustworthy federated edge learning secured by the ledger [P0], co-designed FL-and-blockchain IoT frameworks for edge AI [P1], and resource management for blockchain-enhanced FL in wireless edge networks [P2] — with the composition reaching into metaverse auction mechanisms [P3], multi-objective replication optimization in dynamic cloud-edge clusters [P4], and private-blockchain platforms for secure urban noise monitoring [P5]. The first fielded deployments have now appeared — blockchain-secured FL for industrial air-pollution monitoring with a mechanistic account of data quality [N23], and a federated IoT framework for hospital-building indoor-air-quality and HVAC optimization installed across three healthcare sites [N24] — and reputation itself has moved on-chain, both as a governance mechanism for decentralized federated learning [N6] and as a working demonstration of ledger-backed trust [N9]. Yet none of these systems closes the loop from on-chain conviction to network-level containment: robustness remains orthogonal to the ledger, so a poisoned-but-immutably-logged model is still poisoned.

**Robust aggregation** has three current families. Trust-bootstrapped rules gate each update by its agreement with a server-side reference: FLTrust [B3] bootstraps that reference from a small root dataset and gates with a ReLU-truncated cosine — the direct premise our Lemma 1 formalizes and the strongest classical baseline our evaluation measures. Statistical filtering discards outliers instead: EIFFeL [B5] coordinates per-update integrity checks whose coordinate voting assumes an honest plurality. Reputation-based scoring binds aggregation weight to observed behavior: RAID [N11] scores intrusion-detection clients per round but forgets between rounds, RepuFL [N7] and ReFLect [N8] select mobile-network clients by reputation, and a mean-field-game formulation [N10] prices reputation into incentives — selection and incentive uses that our trust-gated quorum generalizes into a containment-consumable score. Identification goes further than scoring: FedGT [N22] identifies malicious clients under secure aggregation through a hard decision rule, and TFFL/DRC [N21] combines reputation-based consensus with adaptive incentives on-chain. A parallel 2024–2026 wave extends every axis: dual-metric model verification for decentralized vehicular FL with on-chain reputation [N19], loss-based greedy aggregation (FedGreed) [N18], robustness via loss-based client clustering [N20], quantum-secure clustered aggregation [N12], personalization and transfer as the route to robustness under heterogeneity [N13], cryptographic gradient attestation [N14], privacy-preserving Byzantine-robust FL (PPBRFL) [N15], and Byzantine-robust aggregation for decentralized FL [N17]. Each family fails exactly outside its home attack geometry — the finding our Section 5 evaluation quantifies — and all remain orthogonal to the operational trust root we add.

**Federated IDS for healthcare** has been validated across benchmarks — clinical WUSTL-EHMS, the TON IoT telemetry collection [F2], and the large-scale CICIoT2023 attack suite [F4] that serves as our second dataset — but evaluates accuracy only: robustness, when assessed at all, targets label noise rather than the model-layer collusion a network-level adversary actually mounts. ## 1.3 Contributions

This paper presents FedRBC, a blockchain-enabled reputation-weighted robust FL framework for healthcare IoT edge networks, built around one architectural idea: a single on-chain trust root that drives aggregation, selection, and containment together. Our contributions are:

- **C1 — A reference-guided aggregation with adaptive magnitude control.** The RWRA rule (Eqs. 4, 5–7) combines root-direction gating with a median-absolute-deviation (MAD) pre-filter and a relative clipping radius $\tau_c(t) = \kappa_c\|\Delta_{\mathrm{root}}(t)\|$, covering the direction-inconsistent attack classes (sign-flip, Sybil collusion, label-flip) within one mechanism. The aggregation carries a convergence guarantee in which the residual adversarial weight is bounded *explicitly* by the gate and ledger parameters (Lemma 1, Thm. 1) rather than assumed away.
- **C2 — An operational on-chain reputation contract.** The contract (Alg. 2, Eqs. 8–9) renders per-client trust immutable and auditable, and feeds both aggregation weights and client selection; we prove geometric suppression of persistent adversaries (Prop. 1) and a 1/2-approximation for the selection rule (Prop. 2).
- **C3 — A measurable detection-to-containment loop.** Contract events drive SDN quarantine with an M/M/1 mean-time-to-containment (MTTC) analysis (Thm. 2), measured on an emulated testbed.
- **C4 — A thorough simulation-based evaluation.** Against three recent aggregation-level system variants (RAID-style, FedGT-style, EIFFeL 2022) and three canonical classical baselines (FLTrust, Krum, trimmed mean), under three attack classes plus the extended families (LIE collusive poisoning, targeted backdoor scored by attack success rate, reference poisoning, direction-preserving scaling), Byzantine ratios up to 50%, Dirichlet non-IID down to $\alpha=0.1$, and client populations up to $N{=}100$, with ten seeds and Holm-corrected significance tests anchored by centralized and clean-FedAvg references: FedRBC attains the best AUC and the smallest F1 variance in every scenario, an F1 statistically tied with the strongest classical baseline (FLTrust) and significantly ahead of every plurality- or coordinate-based baseline where those families fail (Sybil collusion, Holm-corrected), and it is the only evaluated method that carries trust memory, immutable auditability, and network-level containment; the DP layer of C1's privacy option is measured across three noise levels.

Section 4 presents the framework architecture and design with theoretical guarantees. Section 5 reports the evaluation. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.
